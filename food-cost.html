How to Control Food Cost in a Japanese Restaurant

By a licensed chef in Japan (chōrishi), about 15 years in the food and beverage industry, specializing in Japanese cuisine (washoku). Last updated: November 19, 2026.

Food cost is not controlled in the office. It is controlled at receiving, in storage, on the cutting board and at the pass — every day, in small decisions. This guide gives you a simple system: know your real cost per dish, protect your yield, standardize portions, track waste, and review the numbers every week.

1. Start with the formula

Food cost % = ingredient cost of what you sold ÷ sales × 100

There is no single correct percentage for every restaurant. A ramen shop, a sushi counter and a teishoku restaurant all buy differently and price differently. What matters is that you know your number, dish by dish, and that it matches the price you charge and the labor and rent you also have to pay. Food cost alone never tells the whole story — but without it, pricing is a guess.

2. Cost every recipe, on paper

For each dish, write down every ingredient with its amount per serving. Then work out the cost per serving:

  • Ingredient amount used (g / ml / pieces)
  • Purchase price per unit (per kg / L / pack)
  • Cost for that amount
  • Seasonings, oil, dashi and garnishes — small, but they add up across hundreds of servings

Add the lines up. That total is your recipe cost per serving. Compare it with your selling price before the dish goes on the menu, not after a month of selling it.

3. Test yield — the number most kitchens skip

The price you pay is for the whole ingredient. The part you can serve is smaller. Yield is the usable share:

Yield % = usable weight ÷ purchased weight × 100

Example (illustrative)Weight
Whole fish, as purchased1,000 g
After cleaning, skin and bones removed620 g usable
Yield62%
True cost per 100 g servedPurchase price per kg ÷ 0.62 ÷ 10

The same logic applies to vegetables (peeling, trimming), meat (fat, sinew) and cooked rice or noodles (weight changes with water). If you cost recipes with purchased weight instead of usable weight, your real food cost will always be higher than your paper cost.

Kitchen rule: cost with usable weight, not purchased weight. One yield test per core ingredient is enough to fix most pricing mistakes.

4. Standardize portions

  • Decide a standard portion for every dish and write it on the recipe.
  • Use scales, ladles and portion tools during training until the standard is habit.
  • Photograph the finished plate. A shared image of “correct” prevents quiet portion drift between staff and shifts.

Portion drift of just 10% on a main ingredient is invisible day to day and very visible at the end of the month.

5. Track waste daily, simply

Keep one waste sheet in the kitchen. Each day, record what was thrown away, how much, and why: over-preparation, spoilage, trimming, cooking mistakes, or returned dishes. You are not looking for someone to blame — you are looking for the pattern that repeats. Review it weekly and fix the top one or two causes first: smaller prep batches, better storage rotation (first in, first out), or a menu change that uses an ingredient in more than one dish.

6. Design the menu to help the kitchen

  • Share core ingredients across several dishes where quality allows, so stock turns faster and spoilage falls.
  • Know which dishes carry your margin and which bring in customers — and place them on the menu deliberately.
  • Remove dishes that sell rarely, waste expensive ingredients, or slow the kitchen at peak time.

7. Review every week

  • Sales by dish and total food purchases for the week
  • Your approximate food cost % and how it moved
  • Waste sheet: top causes and what you changed
  • Any supplier price changes — update recipe costs when they happen

Make one or two changes at a time. If you change prices, portions, suppliers and menu all at once, you will never know what worked.

Common mistakes

  • Pricing from competitors’ menus without knowing your own costs
  • Costing with purchased weight instead of usable yield
  • Forgetting seasonings, oil, garnish and rice in recipe costs
  • Checking food cost once a year instead of every week
  • Blaming staff for waste before checking prep quantities and storage
