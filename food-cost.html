Food Cost · Practical Guide 02

How to Control Food Cost in a Japanese Restaurant

By a licensed chef in Japan (chōrishi, 調理師), about 15 years in the food and beverage industry, specializing in Japanese cuisine (washoku, 和食). Last updated: November 19, 2026.

In a working kitchen, food cost is not an accounting topic. It is what happens when the fish is trimmed, when the rice is scooped, when too much was prepped for a quiet Tuesday. This guide is written from the kitchen side: how to calculate food cost correctly, how to cost what you can actually serve — not what you bought — and how to price a menu that still leaves money after labor and utilities are paid.

About the numbers in this article: All prices, costs and sales figures are hypothetical examples, chosen to make the calculations easy to follow. They are not market data and not a promise of results. Use the same formulas with your own supplier prices and your own sales.

Key terms, in plain English

TermJapaneseWhat it means
Food cost %原価率 (genka-ritsu)Ingredient cost divided by sales. The share of each sale eaten by ingredients.
Yield歩留まり (budomari)The usable part of an ingredient after trimming, as a percentage of what you bought.
Edible / usable portion可食部分 (kashoku-bubun)The part a guest can actually eat — no skin, bones, peel or core.
Prep work仕込み (shikomi)Work done before service: cleaning, cutting, marinating, making dashi and sauces.
Lossロス (rosu)Weight or product lost in trimming, cooking, or mistakes — expected to some degree, managed always.
Waste廃棄 (haiki)Food thrown away: spoiled, over-prepped, burnt, or returned. Waste is loss you could have prevented.
Gross profit粗利 (arari)Selling price minus ingredient cost, before labor and other expenses.
Prime cost / FL costFLコストFood cost + Labor cost. In Japan, chefs watch this combined number closely.

1. The correct food cost calculation

There are two food cost numbers, and mixing them up is the most common beginner mistake.

Per dish

Food Cost % (per dish) = Recipe Cost per Serving ÷ Selling Price × 100

This tells you whether one dish is priced correctly. You get the recipe cost from a cost sheet (Section 5).

For the whole restaurant, over a period

Food Cost % (period) = Actual Ingredient Usage ÷ Sales × 100

Use actual usage, not what you purchased. Section 6 shows how to calculate usage from inventory. If the per-dish number and the period number are far apart, something in the kitchen — yield, portions, waste, or unrecorded staff meals — is different from your paper recipes. That gap is where a chef earns their money: finding it.

Kitchen rule: there is no magic target percentage that fits every restaurant. A sushi counter buying premium fish and a noodle shop do not live by the same number. Set your target from your own menu, rent and labor — then hold the kitchen to it.

2. Cost what you can serve, not what you bought

A supplier invoice tells you the price of the whole ingredient. Your guest eats only part of it. Between the two sits yield.

Yield % = Usable Weight ÷ Purchased Weight × 100

True Cost per kg (usable) = Purchase Price per kg ÷ Yield %

Whole salmon — worked example (hypothetical)Figure
Purchase price$28.00 per kg, whole fish
Purchased weight1,000 g
Usable fillet after gutting, skin and bones removed620 g
Yield620 ÷ 1,000 = 62%
True cost of usable salmon$28.00 ÷ 0.62 = $45.16 per kg
Cost of a 90 g portion$45.16 × 0.09 = $4.06

If you had costed that portion at the purchase price, you would have written $2.52 (90 g × $28.00/kg) — understating your real main-ingredient cost by more than a third. Multiply that error by every portion, every day, and a “profitable” menu quietly loses money.

3. How to run a yield test in a real kitchen

  1. Weigh as purchased. Put the whole ingredient on the scale before anyone touches it. Record the weight and the price you paid.
  2. Process it exactly as you do for service. Not more carefully, not less. A yield test only works if it matches a normal busy prep session.
  3. Weigh the usable part. Then weigh the trim separately — the two should add up close to the starting weight.
  4. Calculate and record. Write down: date, ingredient, supplier, purchased weight, usable weight, yield %, and the name of the person who did it.
  5. Repeat three times and use the average. Fish size, season and the person cutting all change the result. One test is a hint; three tests are a standard.
  6. Re-test when anything changes — new supplier, different size, new staff member doing the prep.

Keep a simple yield book or sheet next to the scale. In Japanese kitchens the scale lives in the prep area for a reason: if weighing is inconvenient, it will not happen during service.

4. Loss and waste during prep (shikomi)

Not all loss is the same, and each kind has a different fix.

Type of lossWhere it happensTypical fix
Trimming lossPeeling, skinning, removing bones, fat and damaged partsCannot be zero — build it into yield. Train knife work; use trim where quality allows (stocks, staff meals, minced dishes).
Prep lossOver-cutting, wrong sizes, portions cut too large during shikomiCutting guides, portion scales at prep, sample portions for new staff.
Cooking lossWater and fat leaving during grilling, simmering, fryingCost recipes by served weight where it matters; standardize cooking times and temperatures.
Spoilage & expiry wasteBought or prepped too much; poor storage rotationFirst in, first out (FIFO). Date-label everything at prep. Order and prep to a par level based on real sales, not hope.
Service wasteBurnt dishes, wrong orders, returned plates, dropped foodRecord it, do not hide it. A pattern of mistakes at one station or time of day is a training or layout problem.

How small losses stack up — worked example (hypothetical)

Chicken thighFigure
Purchased10 kg at $8.00 per kg = $80.00
After trimming (12% loss)8.8 kg usable
Over-prepped and discarded at end of service0.5 kg
Actually sold8.3 kg
Effective cost per kg sold$80.00 ÷ 8.3 kg = $9.64 per kg

The invoice said $8.00 per kg. The kitchen really paid $9.64 per kg sold — 20.5% more. Nobody stole anything and nobody was careless in a dramatic way. That is normal, invisible loss, and it is exactly why purchase price alone never tells you your food cost.

Chef's habit: keep one waste sheet on the kitchen wall. Date, item, amount, reason. Review it for two minutes at the weekly meeting. You are looking for the reason that repeats — that one is worth fixing first.

5. Build a cost sheet for every dish

A cost sheet (原価表, genka-hyō) is one page per dish. Every ingredient, costed at usable weight, with the selling price and food cost % at the bottom. If a dish does not have a cost sheet, its price is a guess.

Cost sheet — Grilled Salmon Set (salmon shioyaki teishoku), hypothetical example

IngredientAmount per servingUnit price (usable)Cost
Salmon fillet (from yield test, Section 2)90 g$45.16 per kg$4.06
Rice (raw weight; about 180 g cooked)75 g$2.40 per kg$0.18
Miso soup (miso, tofu, wakame, dashi)1 bowl—$0.29
Pickles (tsukemono)20 g$4.00 per kg$0.08
Grated daikon and lemon wedge1 portion—$0.12
Salt, oil, sake and seasonings1 portion—$0.07
Recipe cost per serving$4.80
Selling price$18.00
Food cost %4.80 ÷ 18.00 = 26.7%
Gross profit per dish$18.00 − $4.80 = $13.20

Notes a working chef adds to the sheet:

  • Do not forget the small lines. Dashi, oil, salt, garnish and pickles look free. Across 100 servings, the $0.07 seasoning line is $7.00, and the miso soup line is $29.00.
  • Write the portion standard on the sheet — “salmon 90 g, cut at prep, weighed” — so any staff member produces the same dish at the same cost.
  • Date the sheet and update it when supplier prices change. An old cost sheet is worse than none, because people trust it.
  • Include a photo of the finished plate. Plating is portion control you can see.

6. Actual ingredient usage: purchases are not usage

For the weekly and monthly numbers, calculate how much ingredient the kitchen actually used:

Actual Ingredient Usage = Opening Inventory + Purchases − Closing Inventory
  • Opening Inventory (期首在庫) — value of ingredients in storage at the start of the period.
  • Purchases (仕入れ) — value of ingredients bought during the period.
  • Closing Inventory (期末在庫) — value of ingredients still in storage at the end. Count what is really on the shelves, in the fridge and in the freezer.
One week — worked example (hypothetical)Amount
Opening Inventory$1,200
+ Purchases this week$2,800
− Closing Inventory$1,000
= Actual Ingredient Usage$3,000
Sales this week$10,000
Food cost %3,000 ÷ 10,000 = 30.0%

Purchases measure shopping; usage measures cooking. Purchases are what you bought — money out, stock in. Usage is what the kitchen actually turned into dishes. They differ because ingredients wait in storage. Buy a month of rice and frozen fish, and purchases spike while usage stays normal. Cook from old stock in a quiet week, and purchases look tiny while usage is real. Judge the kitchen by usage, or a stock-up week will look like failure and a stock-emptying week will look like genius — when the cooking never changed.

Practical tip: do the inventory count at the same time, on the same day, with the same price list, every period. A rough count done consistently beats a perfect count done once.

7. Pricing the menu — and what profit really means

Start from cost, check against the market

Selling Price = Recipe Cost ÷ Target Food Cost %

Using the salmon set from Section 5 (rounded recipe cost $4.80):

Target food cost %CalculationPrice
30%4.80 ÷ 0.30$16.00
25%4.80 ÷ 0.25$19.20

That formula gives you a floor, not a final price. Then a chef checks three real-world things:

  • The guest's view: portion size, quality and what a similar set meal costs nearby. A correct price that feels small on the plate will not sell twice.
  • The kitchen's view: can this dish be produced at peak time, by the staff you actually have, without slowing the pass?
  • The menu's balance: a signature dish can carry a higher food cost % if it brings guests who also order drinks and sides with better margins. Judge the menu as a whole, not dish by dish in isolation.

At $18.00, our example set sits at 26.7% with $13.20 gross profit per dish. That $13.20 is not profit yet — it is the money that must pay the cook, the rent, the gas and everything else in the next section.

Portion is a pricing decision

Raising a price is not the only lever. A salmon portion moving from 90 g to 100 g adds about $0.45 of cost at the yield-tested price ($45.16 per kg) — roughly 2.5 points of food cost on an $18.00 dish — without the guest necessarily noticing or valuing it. Portions decided “by feel” at the cutting board are prices decided by accident.

8. Beyond food cost: labor, utilities and prime cost

A restaurant does not survive on food cost % alone. In Japanese restaurant management, the number watched most closely is FL cost: Food + Labor — internationally called prime cost.

Prime Cost (FL) = Actual Ingredient Usage + Labor Cost

Sales$10,000100%
Ingredient usage (food)$3,00030.0%
Labor (wages for the week)$3,40034.0%

Read the table the way an owner-chef does:

  • Food and labor trade against each other. Buying pre-cut vegetables raises food cost but lowers prep labor. Making everything from scratch lowers food cost but needs skilled hours. Neither is “correct” — the combined FL number is what matters.
  • Utilities are a kitchen cost, not just a bill. A stock pot simmering all day, an oven preheated too early, a fryer held at temperature through a quiet afternoon — these are cooking decisions that appear on the gas and electricity bill. In a Japanese kitchen, when the dashi goes on and when it comes off is a cost decision.
  • A great food cost % can still lose money. At 26% food cost with labor at 45%, prime cost is 71% and there is little left for rent. Watch the pair, never one number alone.

9. The weekly review — a chef's routine

  • Count closing inventory (same day, same time, same price list) and calculate actual usage and food cost %.
  • Check prime cost (FL): usage + labor against sales.
  • Read the waste sheet: which reason repeated? Fix that one first.
  • Compare per-dish cost sheets against the period result. A gap means yield, portions or recording differ from the standard — go and watch that station during service.
  • Update cost sheets for any supplier price changes that week.
  • Make one or two changes only, and note them. Next week you will know if they worked.

Daily habits that protect the week: weigh portions at prep, date-label everything, store first-in-first-out, prep to par levels from yesterday's sales — and taste. A kitchen that stops tasting starts wasting, because seasoning mistakes become remade dishes.

Common mistakes, seen from the kitchen

  • Costing recipes at purchase price instead of usable, yield-tested cost.
  • Forgetting dashi, oil, seasonings, rice and garnishes on cost sheets.
  • Judging a week by purchases instead of actual usage.
  • Setting prices from a competitor's menu without knowing your own costs.
  • Chasing a lower food cost % while labor quietly climbs — and never checking FL.
  • Doing inventory once a year, then arguing about the result for a month.
  • Hiding waste instead of recording it, so the same loss repeats for years.
